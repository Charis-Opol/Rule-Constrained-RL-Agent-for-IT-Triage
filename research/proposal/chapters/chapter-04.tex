\section{Decision Quality}

At the base level of ambiguity, the agent is expected to resolve a larger share of incidents correctly than the initial-symptom, scripted-checklist and always-escalate policies, with a mean investigation cost no higher than that of the scripted checklist (hypothesis H1). The hypothesis is judged by the criteria of Section~\ref{sec:criteria}: a difference of at least 0.05 in resolution accuracy with non-overlapping 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals. The agent is also expected to approach the confidence-threshold policy, which is given the likelihood model that the agent must learn from experience; the gap between the two is reported but is not part of H1. The results will be reported whether or not the hypothesis holds.

\section{Rule Compliance}

The agent with the safety mechanism is expected to achieve a higher compliant success rate than the unconstrained and penalty-only agents (hypothesis H2), by at least 0.05 with non-overlapping confidence intervals. Because a blocked proposal earns the block penalty $\lambda_{\text{block}}$, the proposed-violation rate is expected to fall over training, which would show that the agent learns to act within the rules and does not rely on being stopped.

That no prohibited action is carried out while the mechanism is active is not part of H2, because it holds by construction. It is verified instead: the executed-violation rate of the agent with the mechanism must be zero on every evaluation incident.

\section{Robustness to Ambiguity}

The agent is expected to respond differently to each source of ambiguity, judged one factor at a time from the base level:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Missing evidence (H3a):} as $p_{\text{miss}}$ rises, the agent is expected to escalate more incidents without requesting more checks, since a missing check cannot be requested and there is less evidence left to gather.
\item \textbf{Unreliable evidence (H3b):} as $p_{\text{noise}}$ rises, the agent is expected to request more checks before deciding, to corroborate results it can no longer trust, and to apply fixes on fewer incidents.
\item \textbf{Overlapping evidence (H3c):} as $\omega$ rises, the agent is expected to request more checks and to escalate more incidents, since causes in the same category become harder to tell apart.
\end{itemize}
The agent trained across randomised ambiguity levels is expected to degrade less than the agent trained at a single level. The experiments will report what no reviewed study has reported: how the shares of investigating, fixing and escalating change across controlled levels of missing, unreliable and overlapping evidence.

\section{Pilot Results}

Before the Directorate elicitation, the full design was run on a pilot scenario model of 8 causes, 11 checks and 5 rules supplied by one developer of the Directorate, with fix with verification enabled and three training seeds per agent. The pilot shows that the method runs, learns and can be evaluated as planned; its results are not findings of the study. At the elicited cost of escalation, the learned agents reached the confidence-threshold policy, which is given the likelihood model, and were well ahead of the scripted, initial-symptom and always-escalate policies on return. The safety mechanism kept executed violations at zero, against 7.6\% of incidents for the unconstrained agent. The pilot also exposed two points of the design that the main study will address. Without a cost for a blocked proposal, the agent behind the mechanism continued to propose prohibited fixes in about a fifth of its proposals, so a block penalty $\lambda_{\text{block}} > 0$ will be used as specified. With verification, a checklist that tries one fix after another resolved the most incidents at a low return, so resolution accuracy will be read together with return when hypothesis H1 is judged.

\section{Prototype}

The research is expected to produce a working simulator of IT incident triage built from the Directorate's scenarios, the rule-based safety mechanism, six baseline policies, trained agents, and scripts that reproduce every experiment. Two dashboards accompany them. A training dashboard follows each run and replays an incident step by step, showing the evidence revealed, the agent's estimated action values, any blocked proposal and the final decision. A prototype dashboard runs the decision layer of Section~\ref{sec:prototype} end to end: a ticket and its logs are read, the agent investigates, fixes or escalates under the safety mechanism, and the decision is carried out as a draft pull request on a fictional repository, a fix order, or an escalation routed to the owner of the most probable cause, with every step and its evidence shown.

\textbf{Broader impact.} Putting this together, the expected results address the problem this research is concerned with: that automated triage tools predict an answer for every incident and do not know when to look further, when to act within the rules, and when to hand the incident to a person. An agent that makes these three decisions under explicit, readable rules, evaluated on all four groups of measures, would give IT support teams such as the Directorate evidence on how far such automation can be trusted before it is considered for use.
