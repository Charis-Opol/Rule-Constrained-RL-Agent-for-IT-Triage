\chapterheading{TWO}{LITERATURE REVIEW}

This chapter reviews the literature that bears on the five research questions of the study. It first describes how the literature was gathered and organised. It then synthesises the literature under five themes, one for each research question, and in each theme it separates what the reviewed studies establish from what they leave open. The chapter closes by comparing the studies closest to this one and by stating the research gap that the study addresses.

\section{Review Approach}

The review draws on three bodies of literature that Chapter One identified as relevant: AI-assisted IT incident management, safe and constrained reinforcement learning, and the deferral of decisions from automated systems to humans. A core set of 34 publications was collected across these areas. It comprises surveys of AIOps, empirical studies of incident management, foundational and recent work on shielding and constrained reinforcement learning, studies of selective prediction and learning to defer, and recent work on the runtime governance of language-model agents. Four further sources supply the standard formulations of reinforcement learning, partial observability, costly information acquisition and sequential deferral on which the discussion relies~\cite{sutton2018,kaelbling1998,janisch2019,joshi2023}.

A systematic literature review, conducted for this study and reported separately~\cite{opol2026slr}, supplemented this core set. It followed the PRISMA 2020 guideline. Searches of Scopus, the ACM Digital Library and IEEE Xplore on 23 October 2026 returned 4,399 records. After the removal of duplicates and of records that failed the document-type, year and search-term filters, 131 records were screened on title and abstract, 47 reports were sought, 40 were assessed in full, and 34 studies that apply reinforcement learning or sequential decision-making to IT or security incident handling were included. The studies of that review that bear most directly on this one are discussed in the themes below from their full texts, and its counts are used where they show how common a practice is. Three studies in the core set, by Kim and D\'{a}n, Hammar et al.\ on language-model planning, and D'Antonio and Xie, were not part of the systematic review and are discussed on the basis of their abstracts~\cite{kim2025,hammar2026llm,dantonio2025}.

The synthesis is thematic. Each study was read for what it contributes to one or more of the research questions, and the findings were grouped into five themes that correspond to those questions: the representation of ambiguous incidents, sequential decisions about investigating, recommending and escalating, rule-based safety mechanisms, evaluation measures and baselines, and robustness under ambiguity. The systematic review has its own limits: it searched three databases, its IEEE Xplore search was restricted to open-access records, citation searching was not carried out, and seven reports could not be retrieved~\cite{opol2026slr}. The conclusions of this chapter about gaps are therefore stated for the literature reviewed, and not as claims that the questions have never been examined.

\section{Representing Ambiguous Incident Triage in a Controlled Environment}

The first research question asks how ambiguous triage scenarios can be represented in a controlled environment. The literature answers part of this question by describing the evidence on which triage decisions rest. Remil et al.\ catalogue the data sources of incident management as incident reports, alerting signals, logs, metrics, traces and topology~\cite{remil2024}, and Zhang et al.\ add that language models have brought human-written material such as incident reports and documentation into use~\cite{zhang2025}. Incident reports are the primary source for the assignment of incidents~\cite{remil2024}. An environment for triage must therefore represent two kinds of evidence: the initial report, and the further observations that a member of IT staff could obtain from logs, metrics and other diagnostic sources.

The literature also explains why this evidence is ambiguous. Chen et al.\ found that distinct problems can produce similar symptoms, so that the symptoms alone do not identify the responsible team, and that incomplete knowledge of dependencies and imprecise assessment of resource health delay diagnosis~\cite{chen2020}. Remil et al.\ describe real IT data as commonly noisy and incomplete~\cite{remil2024}. Wu and Gong describe a related condition for agents that act in organisations: the facts needed to judge whether an action complies with policy may be absent from what the agent can see~\cite{wugong2026}. Ambiguity in these accounts has three sources, which are missing observations, unreliable observations, and observations that are consistent with more than one cause. A controlled environment that claims to represent ambiguity should be able to produce each of them.

Realistic incident data is difficult to obtain, and this limits how the reviewed studies represent the task. Chen et al.\ report their results as relative values because of company policy~\cite{chen2020}. Remil et al.\ observe that publishing datasets raises confidentiality concerns, and that most methods for assignment and deduplication have been developed on software bug reports and not on operational incidents~\cite{remil2024}. Studies of sequential decision-making have responded by building or borrowing controlled environments. Dunsin et al.\ built a laboratory of infected virtual machines~\cite{dunsin2025}, Hammar and Li evaluated on the CAGE-2 benchmark~\cite{hammar2025}, Kim and D\'{a}n used simulations on synthetic and real dependency graphs~\cite{kim2025}, and Wang et al.\ combined production data with synthetic datasets containing injected anomalies~\cite{wang2023hrlhf}. Ren et al., in contrast, trained on a fixed dataset of recorded security incidents~\cite{ren2025}. The systematic review found the same pattern across its 34 studies: 13 built their own simulator, six used public benchmarks such as the CAGE cyber-defence gyms and the RCAEval root-cause benchmark, and only three used an organisation's operational data~\cite{opol2026slr}. Simulation is thus an accepted response to the scarcity of data, but the available environments model cyberattacks and microservice faults and not the triage of IT service incidents.

The formal tools for representing uncertainty about an incident are well established. A Markov decision process describes a decision problem in which the agent observes the state~\cite{sutton2018}, and a partially observable Markov decision process extends it to the case in which the agent receives only observations and must maintain a belief about the state~\cite{kaelbling1998}. Several incident-response studies adopt this view. Hammar et al.\ estimate a belief with particle filtering~\cite{hammar2026belief}, Kim and D\'{a}n maintain a belief in a hidden Markov model~\cite{kim2025}, Kovtun et al.\ track a belief over a hidden attack regime in a security operations centre~\cite{kovtun2026}, and Alauthman et al.\ augment the state with recent telemetry and alert history to mitigate partial observability~\cite{alauthman2026}. These studies show that a hidden cause with noisy observations is a workable representation of an incident. They are, however, a minority. Of the 34 studies in the systematic review, only four act on an explicit belief, all of them in security settings, and about half treat the incident state as a fully observed vector of metrics or alert features~\cite{opol2026slr}.

Taken together, the literature supplies the ingredients for representing ambiguous triage but not the representation itself. It identifies the evidence sources, explains the origins of ambiguity, and offers a formal model for hidden causes. What the reviewed studies do not provide is an environment for IT service incidents in which the level of ambiguity can be set deliberately and in which the available actions are those of triage. The first objective of this study responds to that absence.

\section{Reinforcement Learning for Investigating, Recommending and Escalating}

The second research question concerns how reinforcement learning can support the choice among gathering information, recommending an action and escalating. Research on incident triage has so far framed the task mainly as supervised prediction. The methods surveyed by Remil et al.\ range from Naive Bayes classifiers over report text to convolutional and recurrent networks that recommend a responsible team~\cite{remil2024}, and the triage component described by Chen et al.\ is a recurrent model over the incident text and discussion~\cite{chen2020}. One line of this work treats triage as a continuing process and updates its prediction as engineers add to the discussion~\cite{remil2024,chen2020}. The model in that case benefits from new evidence but does not decide to seek it. Zhang et al.\ add that automation in this field mainly stops at triage or the recommendation of solutions~\cite{zhang2025}. Where agents have been placed in the incident workflow, as in the runbook assistant that D'Antonio and Xie studied over 232 production incidents, the agent guides a human operator who carries out the mitigation~\cite{dantonio2025}. The triage literature therefore predicts well-defined labels but does not model the decision about what to do when the label is uncertain.

Reinforcement learning has entered IT operations and incident handling, mostly for diagnosis and response. Ding et al.\ learn a policy that prunes a service dependency graph before root cause analysis~\cite{ding2023}, and Wang et al.\ use hierarchical reinforcement learning to reduce the number of questions put to engineers when that graph is constructed~\cite{wang2023hrlhf}. In cybersecurity, Ren et al.\ train an agent to select response strategies~\cite{ren2025}, Dunsin et al.\ train an agent to conduct a malware investigation~\cite{dunsin2025}, and Alauthman et al.\ propose a deep Q-network agent for incident response in industrial control systems~\cite{alauthman2026}. Hammar and colleagues treat incident response as planning under partial observability~\cite{hammar2025,hammar2026belief}. These studies establish that sequential formulations of incident handling are feasible and can outperform fixed procedures in their own settings. Their action sets, however, consist of responses or investigative steps, and the studies do not address triage as defined in this study.

The decision to gather information has been studied as a sequential problem in its own right. Under partial observability, an action may be chosen for the information it yields, and its value depends on what that information costs~\cite{kaelbling1998}. Janisch et al.\ trained an agent to acquire costly features one at a time before classifying~\cite{janisch2019}. In incident handling, Kim and D\'{a}n choose which alerts to investigate so as to reduce the uncertainty of a belief~\cite{kim2025}, the agent of Dunsin et al.\ collects forensic evidence step by step~\cite{dunsin2025}, and Wang et al.\ learn which questions to ask engineers~\cite{wang2023hrlhf}. Information gathering is consequently a tractable learning problem, and one that has already been applied to parts of incident handling. In these studies the alternative to gathering information is to conclude, and none of them offers the agent the further option of passing the case to a person.

The decision to hand a case to a human has been studied separately, and mostly as a single-step choice. Selective prediction allows a model to abstain when its confidence is low~\cite{geifman2019}, and learning to defer trains a model to pass a case to a human decision-maker by taking that person's ability into account~\cite{madras2018,hemmer2023}. Strong et al.\ note that the standard formulation treats cases as independent and optimises a one-step outcome~\cite{strong2025}. Sequential extensions exist. Joshi et al.\ place deferral in a Markov decision process, where deferring affects later states~\cite{joshi2023}, and Zhang et al.\ formulate deferral as a constrained Markov decision process that accounts for the fatigue of the human expert~\cite{zhang2026falcon}. Guidance on AI risk management likewise lists deferral to a human expert among the configurations in which an AI system may operate~\cite{nist2023}. Applied work treats escalation as an estimate of when a model is likely to be wrong~\cite{bachar2026} or as a decision that policy can make mandatory~\cite{pacella2026}. In each case the choice is between acting and deferring, and the option of first gathering more evidence is absent.

The two lines of work thus stop on either side of the choice this study examines. Information-gathering studies offer the agent two options, to look further or to decide, and deferral studies offer two different options, to decide or to hand over. Hammar et al.\ come closest to joining them: their language-model planner abstains when its candidate actions are inconsistent and then collects external feedback, for example from a digital twin~\cite{hammar2026llm}. That system gathers feedback from a model of the system and not from a human, and its decision rule is a consistency threshold and not a learned policy.

Three studies of security alert triage come closer still, and they are the nearest precedents for this study. Sahin and Mert train an agent that queries logs and threat intelligence and then escalates an alert to incident response or closes it, and a separate conformal layer defers the most uncertain alerts to an analyst under certified error budgets~\cite{sahin2026}. Kovtun et al.\ learn a policy that chooses among shallow triage, escalation to deep analysis and deferral to gather more context, under constraints on analyst capacity and on missed attacks~\cite{kovtun2026}. Okafor trains offline agents that dismiss, investigate or contain alerts, with escalation to analysts fixed in the deployment design and not learned~\cite{okafor2026}. These studies show that gathering evidence and handing over to a person can be learned together. None of them, however, recommends a remedy that changes the affected system: their final actions close, prioritise or escalate an alert. The systematic review confirms how rare even this partial combination is. Of its 34 studies, 17 give the agent an explicit information-gathering action, but only seven include escalation to a person, and only Okafor's combines information gathering, a response action and escalation~\cite{opol2026slr}. Among the reviewed studies, none trains a single policy to choose among investigating, recommending a remedy and escalating, and none does so for IT service incidents.

\section{Rule-Based Safety Mechanisms and Operational Constraints}

The third research question asks how effectively a rule-based mechanism can prevent actions that violate operational constraints. Safe reinforcement learning offers two broad ways to impose constraints on a learning agent. Garc\'{\i}a and Fern\'{a}ndez divide the field into methods that modify the optimality criterion and methods that modify the exploration process with external knowledge~\cite{garcia2015}. Constrained policy optimisation belongs to the first group and bounds the expected cumulative cost of a policy~\cite{achiam2017,wachi2024}. Zhao et al.\ point out that many practical constraints are instead instantaneous and deterministic and must hold in every state~\cite{zhao2023}. An operational rule that forbids a particular action under stated conditions is a constraint of this second kind, which an expected-cost bound does not enforce.

Shielding enforces constraints of that kind by separating the rule from the learner. Wen et al.\ first computed a permissive strategy that satisfies a temporal logic specification and then let the agent learn within it~\cite{wen2015}. Alshiekh et al.\ generalised the idea into a shield that is synthesised from a specification and an abstraction of the environment, and that either restricts the actions offered to the agent or replaces an unsafe action after the agent selects it~\cite{alshiekh2018}. Later work relaxed the requirement of absolute safety. Jansen et al.\ block an action only when it raises the probability of a violation beyond a threshold~\cite{jansen2020}, and Yang et al.\ use probabilistic logic to handle noisy observations~\cite{yang2023}. Hamel-De le Court and colleagues have addressed scalability and uncertainty in the environment model~\cite{hamel2025,hamel2026}. K\"{o}nighofer et al.\ summarise the benefits of shielding as assured compliance with the specification, permissiveness towards safe actions, and preserved convergence of the learner~\cite{konighofer2025}.

Shielding has costs that matter for this study. A shield requires a formal model of the safety-relevant part of the environment, and its guarantee extends only as far as that model and the specification are correct~\cite{konighofer2025}. Pranger and K\"{o}nighofer observe that shielding has seen limited use in practice because it demands expertise in formal methods and considerable engineering effort~\cite{pranger2026}. Yang et al.\ note that a shield which simply rejects actions can hinder learning, because the agent is unaware that its choices were overridden~\cite{yang2023}. The demonstrations in this literature are grid worlds, arcade games, simulated robots and control tasks~\cite{alshiekh2018,jansen2020,achiam2017}. The method is therefore well understood in principle, while its use on the rule sets of IT operations remains largely unexamined.

A parallel body of work enforces explicit rules on language-model agents at run time. Wang et al.\ define a rule language with triggers, predicates and enforcement actions~\cite{wang2025agentspec}, Xiang et al.\ and Chen et al.\ use a second agent to check the actions of the first against stated policies~\cite{xiang2025,chen2025shieldagent}, and Pacella et al.\ place a policy-derived guardrail beneath the agent so that it cannot fall below the escalation that policy requires~\cite{pacella2026}. Wu and Gong check each proposed action against organisational state and return one of three verdicts, to allow, to block or to ask for clarification~\cite{wugong2026}. Hu et al.\ show the scale that rule sets can reach, with a dataset of 12,985 rules drawn from 74 regulations and policies~\cite{hu2026}. This work confirms that rules kept outside the decision-maker can be inspected and enforced independently. It concerns language-model agents and not reinforcement learning, and it does not study how enforcement interacts with a policy that is still learning.

Several studies bring rule enforcement into incident response itself. Alauthman et al.\ couple their deep Q-network agent with an explicit safety shield that enforces operational constraints, in a design where the agent remains advisory by default and the operator can override it~\cite{alauthman2026}. Its setting is cyber-physical control systems, and its agent selects responses without choosing whether to investigate or escalate. The full text reports an evaluation plan and indicative findings shown as directions of change, but no measured values, so it shows that the combination has been proposed and not how it behaves. Hammar et al.\ check the candidate actions of a language model for consistency with system constraints~\cite{hammar2026llm}.

Two studies report measurements of a hard rule mechanism. Singh, in a study of remediation decisions in cloud operations, restricts the agent to a feasible region of actions, adds a deterministic guard before execution, and routes an action to a human when the guard fails~\cite{singh2026}. Removing the guard left the mean time to resolution almost unchanged, at 144~s against 142~s, but lowered the governance compliance rate from 99.9\% to 72.3\%. Ravi et al.\ check every action of an intrusion-response agent for industrial control systems against legal, service-level and safety rules and replace a violating action with a safer alternative or a no-op~\cite{ravi2025}. Their shield intervened between 526 and 623 times per scenario, and the shielded agent took nine to eleven times more false actions than an unshielded one. The two results point in different directions, and neither study reports how often blocked proposals occurred over the course of training, which would show whether the agent learned to avoid them.

The systematic review shows how thinly this evidence is spread. Of its 34 studies, eleven express their operational limits only as reward penalties, which discourage a prohibited action without preventing it~\cite{konighofer2025}, and eight state no constraint at all. Five use a hard filter or shield, and only three say what happens to a blocked or uncertain decision: substitution with a safer action~\cite{ravi2025}, hand-off to a person~\cite{singh2026}, or deferral to an analyst~\cite{sahin2026}. None allows the agent to propose again after a block~\cite{opol2026slr}.

The literature therefore supports the feasibility of an independent rule-based mechanism and identifies the questions that remain. It is established that explicit rules can be enforced on a learning agent without preventing it from learning. It is less clear what should replace a blocked proposal when the agent's alternatives include requesting information and escalating, and how often a learned triage policy would propose prohibited actions in the first place. The third objective of this study addresses these questions for IT incident triage.

\section{Evaluation Measures and Baseline Policies}

The fourth research question concerns how the proposed approach should be compared with baseline policies. Each strand of the literature has its own measures. AIOps research evaluates triage with classification and ranking metrics and evaluates the process with time-based measures such as mean time to repair and mean time to engage~\cite{remil2024}. Remil et al.\ criticise the reliance on contingency-table metrics that ignore context and time~\cite{remil2024}, and Zhang et al.\ report that generated diagnoses and mitigation suggestions often require human assessment because they lack a fixed ground truth~\cite{zhang2025}. Reinforcement learning studies of incident response report the effectiveness of the response, the time taken, and false positive rates~\cite{ren2025}, or the time and accuracy of an investigation~\cite{dunsin2025}. These measures describe whether the final decision was correct and how quickly it was reached.

Studies of constraints and of deferral measure different things. Shielding studies compare a shielded agent with the same agent unshielded and report safety violations alongside reward~\cite{alshiekh2018,jansen2020}. Research on language-model agents has introduced measures that combine the two: Levy et al.\ credit a task only when it is completed without violating any applicable policy, and they find that this measure falls well below the raw completion rate for the agents they test~\cite{levy2026}. Mohammadi et al.\ list compliance with policy among the objectives that agent evaluation in enterprises must cover~\cite{mohammadi2025}. Deferral studies report accuracy as a function of coverage~\cite{geifman2019}, the accuracy of the combined human and model~\cite{madras2018}, or the trade-off between accuracy and the cost of human review~\cite{bachar2026}. Pacella et al.\ measure the recall of mandatory escalations and the stability of the decision across runs~\cite{pacella2026}. Turan relates the escalation rate to realised safety and argues that the relationship need not be monotonic~\cite{turan2026}.

The baselines used in these studies follow a common pattern. Learned policies are compared with fixed rule-based procedures~\cite{ren2025,pacella2026}, constrained agents with their unconstrained counterparts~\cite{alshiekh2018,jansen2020,pacella2026}, and deferring systems with the model alone and the human alone~\cite{madras2018,zhang2026falcon}. Dunsin et al.\ compare their agent with human experts~\cite{dunsin2025}. These comparisons isolate one property at a time, which is the contribution of learning, of the constraint, or of deferral.

The systematic review adds three cautions about these comparisons. First, 20 of its 34 studies compare the learned policy with a static or rule-based one, and in none of the extracted results does the static policy perform better; how much of this reflects the strength of the learned policies and how much the weakness of the baselines cannot be told from the reports~\cite{opol2026slr}. Second, a single metric can hide a degenerate policy. Okafor found that offline agents which had collapsed into trivial detectors still scored well on F1 and ROC-AUC, and that the Matthews correlation coefficient exposed them~\cite{okafor2026}, and the unshielded baseline of Ravi et al.\ reached an accuracy of 0.98 with a recall close to zero~\cite{ravi2025}. Third, a simulator can make a policy look better than it is. Sahin and Mert report that an earlier version of their own simulator leaked the label into the confidence signal, so that any threshold appeared to achieve perfect recall~\cite{sahin2026}. Reporting practice is also weak: only 11 of the 34 studies report results over several runs with a measure of variance, only nine test on conditions not seen in training, and only five report their hyperparameters and release their code or environment~\cite{opol2026slr}.

Decision quality, the cost of investigation, escalation behaviour and constraint compliance are each measured somewhere in the literature, but rarely together. Of the 34 studies in the systematic review, 31 measure decision quality and 19 measure time or investigation effort, but only five measure escalation behaviour and four constraint compliance~\cite{opol2026slr}. One study reports all four: Kovtun et al.\ measure backlog, false escalations, constraint satisfaction and the variance of the policy for escalation in a security operations centre~\cite{kovtun2026}. No study reports all four for the triage of IT service incidents. An agent that can investigate, recommend and escalate could score well on any one of them by neglecting the others, for example by escalating every case or by investigating without limit. The literature thus provides the individual measures and the baseline patterns that this study needs, and it leaves open how they behave when reported together. The fourth objective takes up that question.

\section{Robustness under Ambiguity and Missing Evidence}

The fifth research question asks how robust the approach is as evidence becomes incomplete, conflicting or more ambiguous. The literature treats robustness to imperfect data as a requirement. Remil et al.\ list it among the properties that AIOps solutions must have~\cite{remil2024}, and Mohammadi et al.\ treat robustness to variation as part of the reliability that agent evaluation should probe~\cite{mohammadi2025}. Some studies test it directly. Kim and D\'{a}n report that their investigation policies remain effective under high false-alert rates and investigation errors~\cite{kim2025}, and Hammar and Li evaluate response planning when the assumed model of the incident is wrong~\cite{hammar2025}. Ren et al.\ acknowledge that their agent generalises less well to attack patterns unlike those in its training data~\cite{ren2025}. These results concern the quality of the final decision as conditions degrade.

Uncertainty also affects the mechanisms that are meant to keep an agent within bounds. A deterministic shield assumes that the agent's observations are accurate, an assumption Yang et al.\ describe as unrealistic~\cite{yang2023}, and a shield computed from an estimated model can lose its guarantee when the estimate is wrong~\cite{hamel2026}. Wu and Gong show that a rule cannot be applied correctly when the facts it depends on are hidden from the checker~\cite{wugong2026}. A rule-based mechanism in triage faces the same difficulty, because a rule whose condition refers to the state of a system can only be evaluated on the evidence available.

The human side of escalation is not constant either. Learning when to defer depends on data about the expert that is costly to obtain~\cite{hemmer2023}, people decide differently when they know that a model has deferred to them~\cite{bondi2022}, and expert accuracy changes with workload~\cite{zhang2026falcon}. Turan argues from a modelling study that increasing the escalation rate can reduce realised safety once the reviewer is overloaded~\cite{turan2026}. An agent that responds to ambiguity only by escalating more would therefore transfer the problem and not resolve it.

The systematic review found robustness tests in 15 of its 34 studies, under model error, system change, unseen attackers, weaker detection and dataset shift, and the methods that keep a belief or adapt their model degraded least~\cite{opol2026slr,hammar2025,hammar2026belief}. One result bears directly on escalation. Sahin and Mert show that when the scoring model degrades under dataset shift, the share of alerts deferred to analysts rises, to between 79\% and 99\% for the most affected classifiers, so that failure becomes visible instead of silent~\cite{sahin2026}. That result concerns one decision, deferral, as the scoring model degrades. The reviewed studies have not examined how the balance among investigating, recommending and escalating shifts as ambiguity rises. Robustness has been measured for the accuracy of a decision, for the validity of a shield, and for the reliability of a human reviewer, each in isolation. For a triage agent, the behaviour of interest is the change in its mix of decisions: whether it investigates more, escalates more, or continues to recommend with less support. The fifth objective of this study measures that change across controlled levels of ambiguity and missing evidence.

\section{Synthesis of the Reviewed Literature}

Across the five themes the literature shows a consistent pattern: the components that this study needs exist, but they have been developed and evaluated apart. AIOps research describes the triage task and its evidence but models it as prediction. Reinforcement learning research on incident handling models sequential decisions but concentrates on response and investigation in cybersecurity. Shielding research provides a way to enforce rules on a learning agent but has been demonstrated mainly on control tasks. Deferral research explains when a model should hand a decision to a person but treats the hand-over mostly as a single-step alternative to prediction.

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} compares the studies closest to this one against the capabilities that the research questions concern. The comparison shows that each capability appears in at least one study and that the studies cluster in pairs of capabilities. Sequential policies are combined with information gathering~\cite{dunsin2025,kim2025,wang2023hrlhf}, or with a rule check~\cite{alauthman2026,ravi2025}, or with deferral~\cite{zhang2026falcon}. Rule checks are combined with escalation where the decision-maker is a language model~\cite{pacella2026}. The three studies of security alert triage combine information gathering with escalation, but their constraints are statistical budgets or a severity floor and not rule checks on a remedy~\cite{sahin2026,kovtun2026,okafor2026}. The one study of IT service operations with a rule check and a hand-off to a person, Singh's, neither gathers information nor learns when to escalate; its hand-off is triggered by the rule check~\cite{singh2026}. No row other than the proposed study combines a sequential policy, information gathering, a rule check and learned escalation.

The comparison also qualifies any claim to novelty. A reinforcement learning agent with a safety shield has been proposed for incident response in industrial control systems~\cite{alauthman2026}, and another evaluated in the same setting~\cite{ravi2025}, a deterministic guard with a hand-off to a person has been evaluated for remediation in cloud operations~\cite{singh2026}, agents that gather evidence and escalate or defer have been evaluated for security alert triage~\cite{sahin2026,kovtun2026}, and a planner that checks its actions against constraints and seeks further feedback when uncertain has been evaluated for security incidents~\cite{hammar2026llm}. This study does not introduce the idea of constraining a learned incident-handling agent. Its contribution lies in the particular decision it studies, the setting in which it studies it, and the way it evaluates the result.

\section{Research Gap}

The review identifies five related gaps, one under each research question. The first is a gap in representation. The reviewed studies explain why triage evidence is ambiguous and provide formal models for hidden causes, but they offer no controlled environment for IT service incidents in which ambiguity can be varied and in which the actions are those of triage. The available environments model cyberattacks and microservice faults~\cite{hammar2025,dunsin2025,wang2023hrlhf}, and only four of the 34 studies in the systematic review represent the hidden state of an incident with an explicit belief~\cite{opol2026slr}.

The second gap concerns the decision itself. Information-gathering studies let an agent choose between looking further and deciding~\cite{janisch2019,kim2025}, and deferral studies let it choose between deciding and handing over~\cite{madras2018,joshi2023,zhang2026falcon}. Studies of security alert triage have joined the two, learning to gather evidence and to escalate or defer an alert~\cite{sahin2026,kovtun2026}, but their agents do not recommend a remedy that changes the affected system. No reviewed study trains one policy to choose among investigating, recommending a remedy and escalating, and none addresses IT service incidents in this way. It is consequently unknown whether an agent can learn to treat investigation and escalation as distinct responses to uncertainty when one of its options acts on the system, or whether it will collapse into one of them.

The third gap concerns the interaction between rules and that decision. Rule-based shields have been shown to restrict learned policies in control tasks~\cite{alshiekh2018,konighofer2025}, and hard rule mechanisms have been measured in industrial incident response and cloud remediation~\cite{ravi2025,singh2026}. Only three of the 34 studies in the systematic review report what happens to a blocked decision, none lets the agent propose again, and none reports how often blocked proposals occur over training~\cite{opol2026slr}. The reviewed literature does not report how an independent rule-based mechanism behaves when the constrained agent can also request information or escalate.

The fourth and fifth gaps concern evidence. Decision quality, investigation cost, escalation behaviour and constraint compliance have been measured together for one agent in a single study, of escalation in a security operations centre~\cite{kovtun2026}, and not for the triage of IT service incidents, so the trade-offs among them have not been observed in that setting. Robustness has been tested for decision accuracy, for the validity of shields and for the rate of deferral~\cite{kim2025,hammar2025,hamel2026,sahin2026}, but not for the mix of investigating, recommending and escalating as ambiguity increases.

This study addresses these gaps within stated limits. It will construct a simulated environment for ambiguity-aware IT incident triage, train a rule-constrained reinforcement learning agent to choose among gathering diagnostic information, recommending a permitted action and escalating to a human expert, and evaluate the agent against baseline decision policies on all four measures and across levels of ambiguity. The gaps are stated for the literature reviewed in this chapter. Related work may exist beyond it, and the claim made here is that the reviewed literature leaves these questions open, not that they have never been examined.

\section{Chapter Summary}

This chapter reviewed the literature under five themes that correspond to the research questions of the study. It showed that the literature explains the sources of ambiguity in triage and supplies formal models for it, that sequential decision methods have been applied to incident response and investigation but not to the three-way choice of triage, that rule-based mechanisms can constrain learning agents but have rarely been examined in IT operations, and that evaluation measures and robustness tests exist separately for each strand. A comparison of the closest studies, including those identified by a systematic review of 34 studies, showed that security alert triage has combined information gathering with escalation and that rule mechanisms have been measured in industrial and cloud settings, but that none combines a sequential policy, information gathering, a rule check and learned escalation, and the chapter stated five gaps on that basis. Chapter Three presents the methodology by which the study addresses them.
